1  Introduction

Scientific results depend on the correspondence between a stated method, the experiment that tested
it and the conclusion drawn from that experiment. Automated research makes this correspondence
harder to follow when agents revise a proposal, replace code or reinterpret a result across successive
stages. A proposed addition to an existing model, for example, may become a replacement during
implementation. Its score then supports a different comparison from the one originally planned.

Recent systems combine hypothesis generation, coding, experiment execution and manuscript draft-
ing [1, 2, 3]. They produce many of the artifacts needed to inspect a study, but those artifacts must
remain connected as the study changes. Readers need to recover the source behind a design rationale,
the code and configuration behind a reported result, and the comparisons supporting a component-
level claim. Calls for automated falsification and constraints on autonomous scientists address related
concerns [4, 5]. We call an unresolved mismatch between an accepted claim and its attributed evidence
claim drift. We distinguish evidence-to-idea, idea-to-implementation and experiment-to-claim rela-
tions. Missing records leave a claim unverifiable; they do not by themselves establish a mismatch. A
documented design revision is also not drift when the method description and conclusion are updated
consistently.

XCIENTIST maintains these relations through a research harness (Fig. 1). Its evidence graph records
source passages, method components, limitations, baselines, datasets and experimental conditions.
Literature review and idea generation use these records to frame a problem and propose component
changes. Experiment validation returns to the same records when selecting starting code, controls
and evaluation protocols. This extends literature-grounded idea generation [6, 7] with an explicit
connection to implementation and testing.

Each selected idea is translated into validation contracts specifying inputs, permitted operations,
deliverables and acceptance checks. Workers implement and execute the plan; separate validators
inspect the outputs before a stage advances. Failed checks, repairs and incomplete comparisons
remain in the run record. These contracts apply formal agent-governance principles [8] to the practical
question of what evidence is required to support a research claim.

We evaluate retained records in three settings: training-free memory for LLM agents, graph-structured
traffic forecasting and multiscale physics-informed neural networks. The evaluation combines claim-
level audits with the design histories and task results. Across the nine task-by-relation comparisons,
XCIENTIST had lower audit CDR than EvoScientist, InternAgent-1.5 and AI-Scientist-v2. The case
studies explain the scope of this result: records were more often sufficient to verify claims, while
component attribution and benchmark generalization still required separate checks. The following
sections describe the architecture, results and methods; the appendices retain implementation details,
worked examples and prompt contracts.

The research harness coordinates literature review, idea generation, experiment validation and report
writing. Ideas have identifiable components, each with a role, source attribution, risks and a proposed
test. Search and fusion edit these components, and implementation maps the selected component
list to code. Validation contracts define the baseline, controlled changes and required outputs. A
component-disable switch makes a comparison possible; an executed, matched comparison is still
required to support a contribution claim. Weak or incomplete evidence can prompt a repair or restrict
the conclusion to the complete system.

The system user interface groups runs under research topics and exposes review, ideation and
experiment lanes. The backend stores artifacts, messages, trace events and approval decisions. Users
can inspect failed stages and previous revisions as well as final outputs. Section 5 describes the
implementation of each module.

3  Results

We examine whether the records support the reported claims and how experimental evidence changed
each design. The claim audit compares XCIENTIST, EvoScientist, InternAgent-1.5 and AI-Scientist-
v2; the task evaluations compare the methods produced during each design trajectory with their
task-specific baselines. Major versions denote mechanism changes, and minor versions denote local
refinements, including changes in the memory performance–cost trade-off.

3.1  Motivating example: Claim drift in automated research

  Key Takeaway

  Claim drift is a confirmed mismatch between a claim and its support. Audit CDR counts both
   drifted and unverifiable claims, so its interpretation requires the separate drifted and unverifiable
   counts. A design revision with consistently updated claims is not itself drift.


We first examined three AI-Scientist-v2 runs [2] as diagnostic examples (Table 24). Their retained
records included a proposed plug-in implemented as a replacement model, component roles without
direct tests, and design changes without preserved source links. These cases required two judgements:
whether a support relation was broken and whether the available record was sufficient to decide. We
therefore separated confirmed mismatches from missing evidence before comparing research systems.

We define claim drift as an unresolved mismatch between a scientific claim and its attributed evidential
or experimental basis during research. A claim drifts when it remains presented as supported despite
inconsistent source attribution, design identity or experimental support. A new hypothesis, a negative
result or an explicitly documented revision is not itself drift.
Formally, let τ = (s0, . . . , sT) be a research trajectory and Cacct  the claims accepted as supported at stage
t. For a claim c, Bt(c) denotes its attributed source, design, execution and experimental records. The
set Rt(c) contains the relations required to support its current wording. A relation r(c, Bt(c)) equals
one when the required correspondence holds and zero otherwise. Then

                        Driftt(c) ⇐⇒c ∈Cacct  ∧∃r ∈Rt(c) : r(c, Bt(c)) = 0.                       (1)

The three relation types are evidence-to-idea, idea-to-implementation and experiment-to-claim
(Table 2). Relations are assessed against the corresponding design and claim versions, so a revision
that consistently updates the attribution and validation requirements resolves the mismatch.

An audit can confirm that a required relation holds, confirm that it fails, or lack the evidence needed to
decide. We therefore distinguish supported, drifted and unverifiable claims. A confirmed mismatch takes
precedence over a missing relation. For system s, task q and relation type k, the audit claim drift rate
(CDR) for a non-empty claim set is

                                                      Ns,q,k + Ns,q,k
                                 CDRs,q,k =   Drifted      Unverifiable .                                   (2)
                                               audit             Ns,q,k                                                             Claims

The denominator contains distinct accepted core claims requiring that relation. Under a fixed audit
budget, the unverifiable fraction, NUnverifiable/NClaims, measures how often the available evidence
leaves a claim unresolved; a lower fraction indicates greater auditability. CDR records the combined
fraction with a confirmed mismatch or unresolved support. We report the drifted and unverifiable
counts separately (Table 3). Hereafter, CDR refers to this audit measure.

4  Discussion

Across three computational studies, XCIENTIST had lower audit CDR than each comparison system in
all nine task-by-relation comparisons, with rates ranging from 3.6% to 16.7%. Most of this difference
arose from fewer unverifiable claims. The main observed advantage was therefore that the retained
records more often allowed readers to recover and check the support for a claim. Confirmed-drift rates
varied across systems and settings. These findings place the emphasis on maintaining access to the
evidence needed to assess a changing study, while leaving the correctness of each claim subject to
examination.

Recent systems have extended the automation of idea generation, implementation and experimenta-
tion [2, 3, 12]. As a proposal changes during these activities, its rationale and evaluation must remain
connected to the relevant prior evidence. The shared evidence graph in XCIENTIST provides this
connection by supplying source passages, methodological limitations, baselines and experimental
conditions to both idea generation and validation. A source used to motivate a component can thus
be revisited when deciding how to implement and test it. Retaining the original passage and its
conditions also allows an auditor to distinguish a finding established by prior work from an inference
introduced by the research agent. This distinction matters when a plausible design rationale extends
beyond what the cited study actually demonstrated.

The memory study illustrates how component-based idea generation makes revisions easier to interpret.
Its design evolved from reversible rewriting towards immutable source notes and question-dependent
evidence selection. Representing storage, enrichment and retrieval as separate components allowed the
selection procedure to change while the underlying source notes remained available. More generally,
each component carries its intended role, source attribution and proposed test through search, fusion
and implementation, so an edit can be examined against the assumption it changes. The resulting
memory system improved answer quality and token efficiency, but incomplete component tests limited
attribution of those gains. The component records made this gap explicit even where the complete
system performed better.

Validation contracts connect these proposed component roles to executable comparisons. They specify
the implementation, baseline, controlled component changes and outputs required for a claim, while
separate validators inspect the artifacts produced by workers. Their role becomes particularly clear
when experimental evidence challenges a selected idea. In forecasting, the proposed hard orthogo-
nal projection had little observed benefit under the tested protocol, prompting a revision towards
proposal-space innovation coverage while retaining the diffusion backbone. The contract and com-
ponent mapping provided a way to test the revised operation without losing track of the intended

correction path. In the PINN study, staged coarse–fine learning and the final fixed-complement design
with Fourier features yielded lower mean errors on two benchmarks, while remaining weaker than
external baselines on the two-dimensional multiscale task. These results required benchmark-specific
conclusions despite an improved average rank. Validation therefore affected both the design pursued
and the scope of the claim that could be retained.

The present evaluation does not isolate the contribution of the evidence graph, component represen-
tation or validation contracts to the observed CDR differences. The retrospective audit compared
each system’s own accepted claims, which differed in content and applicability, and depended on the
records available for inspection. The findings characterize these records across three computational
settings; they do not estimate claim-drift incidence across independent research runs. The workflow
also remains dependent on retrieval coverage, the adequacy of the selected comparisons and valida-
tor judgement. Establishing how much each module contributes would require controlled module
comparisons across repeated runs, together with comparable access to the evidence used in each audit.

Transparent reporting requires records that identify the tests, component revisions and evidence
used to delimit a conclusion [22, 23, 24]. In XCIENTIST, the evidence graph, component histories and
validation records serve these decisions and remain available for subsequent review. They allow a
reader to follow a proposed idea through its revisions, identify the implementation behind a result
and determine which completed comparisons support the final claim.

5.1.1  Corpus Construction

Our corpus is drawn from computer science papers indexed through Semantic Scholar [25]. Papers
span multiple CS subfields; the largest domain groups are cs.CL, cs.CV, cs.LG, and cs.AI, with a smaller
tail from neighboring areas. The full graph is constructed from nearly 50K source papers after filtering.


5.1.2  Full-text Structured Parsing

A central design decision is to parse full paper text rather than abstracts alone. Abstract-level con-
tent omits critical signals: baseline comparison details, component-level descriptions, experimental
conditions, and failure modes are rarely recoverable from abstracts but are essential for method-level
evidence extraction. We parse each paper using MinerU [26], which merges the rendered Markdown
text with structured content lists (such as tabular data) into a unified representation. This ensures that
empirical evidence from tables is preserved and directly available to the extraction stage.

Paper-level metadata include year, venue, title, domain label, a structured summary of the background,
method and results, and a code-repository URL when available. These records are shared by all core
entities extracted from the paper.


5.1.3  Schema-bound Evidence Extraction

Given a research topic, DeepSurvey retrieves seed papers via the Evidence Graph mentioned in
Section 5.1 with Semantic Scholar API as fallback and expands along citation and reference edges in
both directions to a bounded depth. This graph-based expansion captures inter-paper dependencies
that flat semantic search misses, producing a structurally connected evidence collection.

A title/abstract similarity filter removes weak candidates at low cost. An LLM then checks relevance
against the topic and seed context. The retained set supplies the full-text analysis stages.


5.2.4  Full-text Keynotes

For each retained paper, DeepSurvey generates a structured keynote from the full text rather than
from the abstract alone. This design choice is motivated by the observation that key technological
details—implementation mechanisms, experimental configurations, applicability conditions, and
failure modes—are often absent from or heavily condensed in abstracts, yet are essential for meaningful
cross-paper comparison and mechanism-level reasoning.

Keynotes adopt an open-ended schema that adapts to different contribution types (method, system,
or theory papers) without imposing rigid categorical templates. Each keynote records contributions,
methods, experiments, assumptions, and limitations in a domain-adaptive format. The resulting
keynotes preserve the contribution-specific details used in later comparisons.

5.2.5  Clustering and Multi-perspective Relation Analysis

After keynote generation, DeepSurvey organizes papers into thematic clusters. A clustering agent
designs cluster themes from the complete keynote collection, and a partitioning agent assigns papers
accordingly. Papers spanning multiple topics may be assigned to multiple clusters, reflecting the
cross-topic nature of complex contributions.

Within each cluster, three complementary analytical perspectives are applied:

  • Relation graphs model typed citation relationships (e.g., foundation, extension, substitution,
     complementarity) with abstracted descriptions, capturing technical lineage and method evolution
       trajectories.
  • Comparison tables align papers along key technical dimensions—method objectives, core
     mechanisms, experimental setups, and limitations—enabling systematic horizontal comparison
     within a research theme.
  • Guided Q&A synthesis integrates cluster evidence to answer high-value research questions,
     producing compact analytical results that support cross-paper pattern extraction and gap identi-

A code-analysis agentic subsystem extends the textual analysis to source-code repositories, retrieving
implementation-level details and exposing technological dimensions overlooked by paper prose alone.
Inter-cluster analysis is also performed to capture cross-cluster differences and broader research
landscape patterns. All analytical outputs require explicit source attribution, with checks for missing
or unsupported source attribution.


5.2.6  Outline-driven Drafting and Citation Enforcement

DeepSurvey generates a hierarchical outline from the clustering results and iteratively refines it against
retrieved paper keynotes. Papers are then explicitly assigned to appropriate sections based on their
keynotes and cluster analyses. This citation-anchoring process creates a localized evidence constraint:
each writing unit depends only on its assigned paper subset, reducing hallucination risk by limiting
the context available to the generation model.

Writing proceeds bottom-up: subsection drafts receive the outline, relevant cluster analyses, and
assigned paper keynotes; section drafts aggregate subsection content with overview-level analysis.
After each paragraph, a citation verification mechanism checks consistency and authenticity against
the assigned paper set. Detected errors trigger retry generation with error memory, enforcing strict
alignment between generated text and the evidence collection.


5.2.7  Multi-granularity Agentic Refinement

After initial draft generation, DeepSurvey applies iterative refinement at three granularities: section-
level (organization and logic), subsection-level (local analysis, readability, and citation-claim valida-
tion), and survey-level (cross-section coherence). A centralized planning agent inspects the draft,
outline, revision history, and relevant evidence, then coordinates specialized roles—keynote reader,
reviewer, and reviser—through an explicit action plan. Global memory preserves reviewer feedback,
score changes, and revision states across rounds, ensuring cross-round consistency. A simplified
skill-loop fallback is available for weaker backbone models.

5.3.3  Memory-Guided MCTS

We model idea generation as a Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) procedure over structured idea states.
Starting from a root idea, XCIENTIST expands child candidates, evaluates them through a referee-
style simulation step, and backpropagates the resulting reward along the search path to guide future
selection. The Select and Backpropagate phases follow standard MCTS, so the key system-specific
design lies in the Expand and Simulate operations.

Expand.  Unlike prior work that treats ideation as largely free-form generation [3, 12], XCIENTIST
uses a structured action space during expansion. It treats each idea as a set of editable components
and applies component-level operators such as adding, removing, replacing, or rewiring modules,
as well as introducing validation protocols. These operations are composed into a higher-level edit
skill S, allowing XCIENTIST to perform targeted architectural revisions while keeping the search space
interpretable and experimentally grounded.

To make expansion adaptive to past search experience, we further introduce a vector-memory mecha-
nism. When XCIENTIST receives feedback during simulation, it stores that experience and retrieves
relevant signals in later expansions. The retrieved memory provides local edit patterns, failure correc-
tions, or validation hints observed in other search trajectories, thereby guiding the generation of new
child ideas:
                                                                                                               (5)                                                  Iil = Expand(Sil, Ijl−1, Mv | taste),
where Iil denotes the i-th idea at layer l, Sil denotes the instantiated edit skill for that idea, and Mv
denotes the retrieved vector memory. In effect, XCIENTIST implements an implicit form of cross-over:
instead of explicitly merging two parent ideas, it reuses and recombines useful local patterns from other
branches. Cross-domain transfer is handled within this expansion stage by retrieving mechanisms
from other fields only when they address a diagnosed weakness in the current idea and can be adapted
back into the target domain.


Simulate.  For each candidate idea, XCIENTIST builds an evaluation prompt from the topic, fixed
root-domain constraints, the root-idea anchor, the candidate method description, and retrospective
symbolic-memory hints. The evaluator then produces a multi-dimensional judgment over novelty,
surprise, impact, feasibility, clarity, alignment, complexity, risk, and protocol quality. These signals
are aggregated into a composite score used for MCTS backpropagation and search control. We use
symbolic memory here as a grounded hint because it is derived from component-wise ablation results
obtained during experimental validation, which allows XCIENTIST to reason about the functional role
each component played in prior ideas:

                                   {ril, f li } = Simulate(Iil, I0, T , Ms | taste),                                (6)

where ril and f li denote the reward and verbal feedback for the i-th idea at layer l, T denotes the topic,
and Ms denotes the retrieved symbolic memory. Novelty is assessed in a reference-grounded and
mechanism-centered way: rather than relying on surface-level difference, the evaluator compares the
candidate against nearby prior work in the evidence graph and assesses the proposed component
change.

figure{N}.png. The method file serves as a structured prompt that describes the visual concept, main
elements, and key message, ensuring that generated images are aligned with the article content rather
than decorative. After generation, the <graphN> placeholders in blog_article.md are replaced with
standard Markdown image tags, producing a self contained article with embedded figures.


5.5.6  Checkpointing and Resumability

After each stage, workflow_status.json records completion. An interrupted workflow resumes from
the last completed stage.

The final output is an article accompanied by an audit report and figures. Its inputs remain available
under the experiment workspace, including project/, result files and iteration reports. Unresolved

experiment validation.

5.7  Case-study protocols

We evaluated the research harness in three settings chosen to vary the amount and type of prior
constraint. Each run began from an existing method, retrieved task-specific evidence and evaluated
successive component-based designs against that starting point. The training-free memory task
provides a relatively open design space. Graph-structured traffic forecasting adds a fixed backbone,
spatial structure and task-specific robustness tests. Multiscale physics-informed neural networks add
governing equations and coarse–fine constraints that restrict both admissible designs and evaluation.
Detailed version-level results are reported in Table 22.


5.7.1  Training-free memory for LLM agents

The memory case began from an A-Mem implementation [15] and evaluated versions on the same
subset of LoCoMo [16]. Evidence retrieval focused on provenance preservation, long-context failure
modes, retrieval reliability and prompt cost. The search space was restricted to training-free memory
operations and retained the language model as an external generator. The selected idea comprised
immutable atomic-note memory, deterministic write-time enrichment, sparse handle support, slotted
evidence retrieval and a deduplication guardrail.

The baseline, complete candidate and available component-disabled conditions used a shared exe-
cution path. Embeddings were generated with all-MiniLM-L6-v2, and answers were generated with
gpt-4o-mini. Evaluation used category-level textual F1 for single-hop, multi-hop, temporal, open-
domain and adversarial questions, together with overall textual F1 and average token length per query.
Baseline and complete-candidate comparisons used matched data, embedding and answer-generation
settings.

5.7.2  Graph-structured traffic forecasting

The traffic-forecasting case began from Graph WaveNet [17] and the PEMS-BAY dataset [18]. Evidence
retrieval prioritized diffusion-based backbones, spatial attention designs, missing-data robustness and
horizon-specific evaluation. The intended design space retained the diffusion backbone as the main
predictor and restricted changes to residual branches and their interfaces. The initial replacement
candidate departed from that boundary; subsequent revisions restored the backbone. Its retained
result is reported as part of the design history, not as evidence for a backbone-preserving extension.

The standard comparison evaluated Graph WaveNet and successive candidates on clean PEMS-BAY
forecasting. Performance was measured by mean absolute error averaged across horizons and at 15-,
30- and 60-min horizons. Robustness was evaluated under encoder-side block missingness, including
the block-40% condition. Component tests removed or replaced the proposed projection or coverage
operation while holding the backbone and remaining residual path fixed. These tests were used
to determine whether the corresponding component changed clean forecasting error or robustness
degradation under the declared protocol.


5.7.3  Multiscale physics-informed neural networks

The physics-constrained case addressed multiscale heat equations with physics-informed neural
networks. Evidence retrieval focused on spectral bias, optimization coupling, coarse–fine interference
and prior multiscale PINN designs. Candidate ideas were required to remain trainable under partial-
differential-equation, boundary-condition and initial-condition losses. The search space was further
restricted so that the coarse branch represented the global solution and residual branches supplied
fine-scale corrections without overriding the coarse prediction.

Successive candidates were evaluated on heat1d_multiscale, heat2d_multiscale and pinnacle_-
heat [19]. External comparisons included MMPINN [20] and MultiscalePINNs [21]. The primary
outcome was mean relative L2 error with the reported variation for each task.

Average rank across the three settings summarized version-level progress without replacing the per-
task results. Validation also compared progressive and simultaneous residual activation and checked
whether residual branches preserved the designated role of the coarse backbone. Conclusions were
assessed separately for each benchmark so that improvement in one- or two-dimensional settings was
not generalized across tasks without supporting results.

B.2  Analysis of Field Structure

On top of the retrieved papers, XCIENTIST performs an advanced analysis that separates the field into
methods, consensus, open problems, and evaluation gaps. The analysis identifies several recurring

method families: structured textual and procedural memory with explicit schemas, non-parametric
cheatsheets or distilled rule memories, verbal reflection and lesson memory, dense-plus-lexical re-
trieval with compact packing, latent or KV-based working memory for efficiency, and verification or

evidence packing, runtime efficiency, and reliability control as distinct design axes.

The field consensus extracted by XCIENTIST further constrains the design space. Training-free external
memory is preferred when online update, auditability, and deployment simplicity matter. Long-term
memory should store compressed, high-signal artifacts rather than raw interaction transcripts. Read
latency and prompt budget are core constraints, so retrieval should be multi-stage and evidence
packing should be compact and decision-oriented. The analysis also concludes that semantic similarity
alone is insufficient when wrong recalls are costly; reliability, provenance, temporal fit, and context fit
should participate in retrieval decisions. These consensus statements narrow the search space away
from heavier training-based or latent-memory solutions and toward explicit, inspectable retrieval
rules.

B.3  Problem Diagnosis

The same analysis identifies the main failure modes of existing approaches. Memory retrieval remains
too similarity-first and heuristic; admission, deduplication, and refinement often depend on brittle
thresholds; memory growth creates duplicates, contradictions, and bloat; and evidence packing
remains vulnerable to redundancy and order sensitivity. Temporal and provenance metadata are useful
but noisy, and many systems lack retrieval rules that benefit from these signals without collapsing
when they are absent. The analysis also incorporates prior ablation evidence: additional slotted
reranking, separate handle indexing, and minority-aware provenance adjudication do not show clear
marginal value in the observed setting. As a result, the system avoids proposing another auxiliary
reranker or index. Instead, it redirects the idea toward a simpler and more testable retrieval principle.

B.4  From Evidence to Root Idea

The resulting mature idea is Family-Scoped Expected-Utility Retrieval for Atomic Note Memory. The idea
retains immutable span-grounded atomic notes as the durable memory unit, but shifts the center
of the memory system from auxiliary indexes and reranking gadgets to family-scoped, reliability-
aware retrieval. Each note is lightly enriched with embeddings, confidence-tagged entities and time
expressions, compact QA keywords, short context descriptors, and a provenance-family identifier.
Retrieval remains a bounded ANN plus lexical/facet screening stage, followed by deterministic rank-
and-pack. The key change is that candidates are scored not only by query relevance, but also by
temporal fit, provenance corroboration, and family reliability. Duplicate handling is simplified into
family quotas and soft duplicate decay inside packing.

The resulting proposal links each retrieval change to the retrieved analysis or prior component-test
record. It replaces similarity-first ranking with family-scoped expected utility while retaining atomic
notes and bounded candidate screening.

XCIENTIST treats fusion as a component-selection problem: it identifies which components carry the
central novelty, which components should remain as support infrastructure, and which components
should be rejected because they introduce unnecessary control complexity.

The fused draft selects the moonshot inventor candidate as the host idea and forms the thesis Analytic
Family-Calibrated Rank-and-Pack for Training-Free Atomic-Note Memory. The selected core mechanism is
CalibratedBayesianFamilyRankAndPack, supported by ShrinkageAnalyticNullEstimator. The former
provides the main read-time family-aware calibration mechanism, while the latter supplies a closed-

same time, the fusion preserves mature infrastructure from other candidates: two_stage_retrieval
keeps the retrieval path bounded and efficient, modular_atomic_note_enricher provides the metadata
needed by the calibrator, and lightweight guardrails such as quota decay and note-attached micro-

G  Final Designs and Idea Process Across Three Tasks

G.1  Task-specific designs produced by XCIENTIST

The three research tasks led to designs with different constraints on what could change and what had
to remain identifiable (Figure 16). In memory, the design separated preserved source notes from the
evidence selected for an answer. In graph forecasting, it retained a diffusion backbone while revising
the operation that supplied local corrections. In physics-informed neural networks, it separated a
learned coarse solution from later fine-scale residuals. These component boundaries connected the
proposed design to its implementation and defined the comparisons needed to assess its contribution.


Training-free slotted evidence retrieval.  The memory design preserved immutable atomic notes
as the authoritative evidence and organized retrieval around the needs of a question (Figure 16a).
Lightweight enrichment attached cached embeddings and compact entity, time, keyword and prove-
nance information without replacing the source note. A bounded first-stage screener combined
approximate-nearest-neighbour retrieval with lexical overlap and optional entity or time filtering. A
deterministic slotted reranker then assembled a capacity-capped bundle with roles for direct support,
provenance, temporal context and conflicting evidence. Sparse facet handles supplied optional ranking
boosts, while the deduplication guardrail limited repeated-source contributions and retained bounded
minority contradictions. This arrangement made the selection and packing of evidence editable while
keeping the underlying record available for inspection. The matched LoCoMo comparisons in the
main text supported improved answer quality and reduced token use for the complete system.


Diffusion-referenced innovation coverage for graph forecasting.  The final forecasting design
retained Graph WaveNet as the main predictor and confined the proposed addition to a local correction
path (Figure 16b). Gap-adaptive cached prior filling supplied the encoder input, and the observed
mask supplied a reliability bias. Sparse edge attention operated within a fixed top-k neighbourhood
to produce a correction proposal. The innovation coverage cell scaled that proposal according to its
alignment with the state left unexplained by the diffusion response, before combining the matched
correction with the backbone and residual path. The resulting design made the contribution of
the coverage operation testable without replacing the forecasting backbone. It followed an earlier
projection-based proposal, whose removal produced only a small change in clean-data error under
the evaluated protocol. Subsequent component tests supported retaining coverage on PEMS-BAY,
alongside the final system-level improvement. The projection proposal shown in Figure 17b therefore
belongs to an earlier design stage than the coverage-based endpoint shown here.


Staged coarse–fine learning with a fixed complement.  The PINN design assigned the broad solution
and its fine-scale corrections to separate stages (Figure 16c). A coarse global branch was first trained
using the partial differential equation and boundary and initial conditions, and was then frozen. Later
additive branches produced residual functions from which a fixed projector removed the designated
coarse contribution before reconstruction. The derivative-consistent formulation was intended to
maintain this separation when the reconstructed solution was evaluated through the physics loss. The
final experimental version also enriched the coordinate inputs with Fourier features. The retained
evaluations supported lower mean error on the one-dimensional multiscale and PINNACLE heat
tasks, while external baselines remained better on the two-dimensional multiscale task. The fixed-
complement design thus specified how the branches should interact; its performance claim remained
conditional on the benchmark results.

G.2.2  Graph forecasting: making residual complementarity testable

The forecasting root proposed residual correction without a sufficiently explicit account of how it
would complement diffusion (Figure 17b). The displayed ambitious-realist search progressed through
